I spent 75 minutes with the Women in Leadership class at CU Boulder's Leeds School of Business, walking first-year students through the networking ideas they'd just read in Herminia Ibarra and Mark Hunter's _How Leaders Create and Use Networks_.

Most of the students were freshmen, about half from business and half from engineering. They don't have networks yet in the career sense, and I did not want to hand them a blank three-circle diagram and tell them to go build one. So the whole session ran on a reframe: **your network is the people you already know, you just haven't reached out to them yet.**

**How it went**

- I told one story about entering tech through a nontraditional path (non-profits, marketing, support, then software engineering) and how the network followed me rather than staying behind with the field.
- The giving beat: I wanted to grow a network fast and didn't know how, until I saw someone doing community work and offered to help. Asking is easy when you're already the one holding the door open.
- The honest "what's next": AI is reshaping my role and I'm figuring out where I want to grow next. I asked the room live for anyone who knew someone in robotics or cybersecurity, which flips the power dynamic and shows them they already have something to offer.
- Then their turn.

**The exercise**

The students worked from the three reach-outs:

1. Who haven't I thanked lately, who went out of their way for me?
2. Who in my work life could I get to know better?
3. Who in my personal life could I learn more about what they do?

For each, they wrote a real name and the first line they'd actually send, then circled the one they'd send that week. We closed with a live intro brokering bit, connecting students in the room to people they'd just realized they wanted to meet.

The whole point: they left with three concrete, low-stakes reach-outs instead of an abstract diagram. The act of reaching out _is_ the networking, and it doesn't require an event.
